# The Public Clock and the Working Day in Marrowby, 1840 to 1880 (invented test reading, pages 101 to 103)

[p. 101] Before 1850 the people of Marrowby kept time by the mill bell, which rang at six, noon and seven. The author argues that the bell was a signal of the mill owner's authority rather than a shared measure of time. Records show that in 1846 three weavers were fined for "disregarding the bell".

[p. 102] The town council installed a public clock on the corn exchange in 1853. Council minutes record that the clock cost 212 pounds and was paid for by subscription from shopkeepers. The author claims the clock was intended to move authority over time from the mill to the town, but concedes that the minutes never state this purpose.

[p. 103] After 1860 the railway timetable required the clock to be set to a single standard. The author notes that the mill bell continued to ring until 1874, and suggests, without evidence beyond one diary, that some households preferred it.
